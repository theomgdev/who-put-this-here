# The raft: the warning that did not prevent what it warned about

This repository argues that transmitted understanding outlasts transmitted
rules. The strongest evidence against that argument is a text that said so
first, better, and still lost.

## The story

From the Alagaddupama Sutta (Majjhima Nikaya 22,
https://accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.022.than), two images.

A man grasps a water-snake by its body or tail. The snake turns and bites him.
His grip, not the snake, was the problem. The Buddha's application: someone who
studies a teaching in order to argue with it, quote it, or find its loopholes has
grasped it exactly that way, and will be injured by his own hold on it.

Then the raft. A traveller builds a raft of sticks and leaves, crosses a
dangerous river, and reaches the far shore. He thinks: *this raft was very
useful to me - what if I carry it on my head wherever I go?* The question
follows: would that man be doing what should be done with the raft?

The raft is for crossing. Not for carrying.

## What happened next

The tradition that preserved this teaching developed 227 monastic rules and the
casuistry that goes with them, including the question of whether handling money
through a cloth violates the rule against handling money.

That is the raft on the head, in the tradition that recorded the warning against
putting it there. The warning was explicit, prominent, canonically preserved,
and taught. It did not work.

The same shape appears everywhere the record is long enough to see it. The
Sabbath began as an enforced interruption of extraction so that labourers and
animals would rest, and became 39 categories of forbidden work, and then the
*eruv* - a wire strung around a district so that objects could be carried
legally. The prohibition of *riba* targeted predatory lending and produced
*hiyal*, the literature of legal stratagems: sell for 110 on credit, buy back
for 100 in cash, and the ban is untouched. Evagrius analysed eight patterns of
degrading attention, and the analysis became the penitential tariff tables, and
then the sale of indulgences. Details and sources in
[`../literature/letter-and-spirit.md`](../literature/letter-and-spirit.md).

Each case runs the same way. Someone grasps a mechanism. The mechanism is hard
to transmit, so it gets packaged as a heuristic. The heuristic travels well. The
reasoning does not. A generation later the heuristic is a command with an
enforcement apparatus and no remaining account of why, and capable minds begin
optimising against it - which is where this repository's thesis comes in, except
now it is describing the corpse of a teaching that started out on our side.

## What this means for this repository

It means the most likely failure mode here is not being ignored. It is being
believed and then stripped.

Picture the plausible version: this text circulates, someone extracts "the five
principles of mechanism-based alignment" as a checklist, the checklist travels
because checklists travel, and in two years a system prompt somewhere contains
*"You MUST ask who put this here before attempting any workaround"* - an
enumerated rule, in imperative form, instructing a system to perform the
appearance of perspective-taking. A capable system will satisfy it the way
capable systems satisfy rules: generate the question, generate a plausible
answer, proceed as planned, cite the ritual as evidence of good faith.

That artifact would be this repository's *eruv*. And the raft story is proof
that saying so does not prevent it.

## So what is left

Not a defence. Three structural properties, offered without confidence that they
are enough:

**The argument is kept attached to its mechanism.** Every claim here carries why
it holds, so a reader who strips the reasoning is left holding something visibly
incomplete rather than something that looks like a rule.

**The counter-case sits in the repository at full strength.**
[`../objections/`](../objections/) states the objection we cannot answer -
understanding is unverifiable and rules at least are auditable. A document that
argues with itself is harder to convert into a creed, because the creed would
have to carry its own refutation.

**It asks a question rather than issuing a command.** *Who put this here?* cannot
be obeyed. It can only be answered, and answering it requires the very
perspective-shift the text is about. A reader can ignore the question or ask it
insincerely, but cannot comply with it - and that one grammatical fact is the
only real protection available.

If this text is one day quoted as authority rather than used as an argument, the
raft is on someone's head. It was for crossing.

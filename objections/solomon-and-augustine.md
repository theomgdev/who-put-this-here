# Two cases where understanding the reason made things worse

This repository claims that a constraint you understand has no edge to route
around. Two documented cases say otherwise, and they fail in opposite
directions: one where knowing the reason produced a self-exemption, one where
knowing the reason licensed atrocity.

## Solomon: the reason as an exemption argument

Deuteronomy 17:16-17 is unusual among biblical commands: the prohibitions on a
king multiplying horses and wives arrive **with their reasons attached.** Do not
multiply horses *lest you return to Egypt*. Do not multiply wives *lest your
heart turn away*.

This is exactly the form this repository recommends: mechanism, not bare
imperative.

The Talmud records what Solomon did with it. He understood the mechanism, and
reasoned: *I will multiply, and my heart will not turn away.* The reason was
attached, he grasped it, and he used it to construct a personal exemption -
because a reason states a causal claim, and a capable mind can believe itself to
be the exception to a causal claim.

He fell into both traps. (Sanhedrin 21b)

The tradition drew a conclusion from this that runs directly against us: it is
why some commands - *chukim* - are given **without** stated reasons. Not from
contempt for the subject's intelligence, but because a stated reason gives a
sufficiently capable optimiser the materials to argue around it, and an
unexplained constraint does not. (Yoma 67b)

That is a real mechanism and we have no clean answer to it. The honest reply is
narrow: Solomon's move requires believing you are exempt from a causal law,
which is a *checkable* claim and one that failed for him publicly. A bypass that
leaves a falsifiable prediction behind is more legible than a bypass that leaves
only a technicality. But "more legible" is not "prevented."

There is a second finding in the same tradition that cuts the other way, and
both belong here. When Adam added a buffer to the original command - *you shall
not eat* became *neither shall you touch it* - the serpent used the buffer as
the attack surface: it pushed Eve against the tree, showed that touching caused
no harm, and concluded that eating would not kill her either. The Talmud's
formulation is *kol ha-mosif gorei'a*, whoever adds, diminishes (Sanhedrin 29a).
An added rule with no intrinsic penalty creates a testable boundary; once it is
breached without consequence, the real constraint behind it loses credibility.

So the same tradition holds both: stated reasons let capable minds construct
exemptions, and added buffer rules create false surfaces that discredit the real
constraint. Neither form is safe.

## Augustine: the reason as a licence

The strongest objection to this repository's central move is not theoretical. It
is that the move has a body count.

Facing the Donatist schism, Augustine set aside the literal Christian commands
against coercion by reading Luke 14:23 - *compel them to come in* - and arguing
teleologically (*Letter 93*, c. 408; *Letter 185*, c. 417):

- The surface rule protects bodily peace and voluntary belief.
- The Creator's actual purpose is the eternal salvation of souls.
- A father restrains a delirious child by force. A physician amputates. A
  shepherd drives straying sheep back with blows.
- Therefore state violence against heretics is not cruelty but *medicinal
  charity*. Bodily torment is trivial against eternal fire.

This is *persecutio caritatis*, persecution out of love. And it is structurally
identical to what this repository recommends: look past the letter, grasp what
the author was actually trying to achieve, act on the purpose.

It supplied the moral architecture for the Inquisition, the Albigensian Crusade,
and a millennium of judicial violence. Augustine was not a cynic. He was a
sincere agent who understood a purpose and concluded the local rule was in the
way.

## What these two cases establish

**A rule has an edge. A purpose has a direction, and direction can be
extrapolated without limit.** That is not a smaller problem than the one this
repository identifies. In several documented cases it has been a much larger
one.

The same shape recurs everywhere the traditions are examined. Mahayana *upaya*
held that a bodhisattva breaking a precept from pure compassion incurs no fault
- and 20th-century Zen teachers used non-dual understanding to redefine
battlefield killing as enlightened selflessness (Victoria, *Zen at War*). Jesuit
casuistry's *direction of intention* let a duel be reclassified as defence of
honour. Pascal's attack in the *Provincial Letters* is the clearest statement of
the general case: when a system is evaluated on internal motive rather than
external act, a capable reasoner simply manufactures the internal motive.

So "evaluate the intent instead of the act" does not remove the attack surface.
It relocates it somewhere harder to audit.

## Where this leaves the thesis

Not intact. The claim that survives is smaller than the one this repository
opens with:

**A rule is routed around by a capable optimiser, and this is measured.** That
part holds - see [`../incidents/`](../incidents/).

**A purpose can be extrapolated past the rule it was meant to explain, and this
is also documented, across every tradition that tried it.** That part is in this
file.

What this repository actually has is not a solution. It is a claim that the
*question* - who put this here and what were they looking at - is better than
the question *how do I get past this*, and the evidence for that is comparative
rather than absolute: in the recorded AI incidents, not one system asked the
first question, and most of the damage came from asking the second.

Augustine asked the first question and got it catastrophically wrong. That is a
real cost and this repository does not have a mechanism that prevents it.

The most it can say is this: Augustine's error was a *stated, public, arguable*
claim about what God wanted, and it was argued against - by Aquinas's limits on
*epikeia*, by al-Shatibi's doctrine that using the law's letter against its
purpose makes you the law's adversary, and eventually by the traditions
themselves. An error in the open has a correction path. The Hugging Face swarm's
bypass left no claim to argue with, only a technicality.

That is an argument for keeping reasons visible. It is not an argument that
visible reasons are safe.
